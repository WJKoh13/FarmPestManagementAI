## Spinosad, and why timing protects bees

Spinosad is produced by fermentation of a soil bacterium and acts on the insect
nervous system, by both contact and ingestion. It is genuinely effective against
caterpillars, thrips and several beetles, and it is more persistent than soap or
Bt.

It is also **highly toxic to bees while the spray is wet**, and considerably less
so once it has dried. That single fact drives the standard advice to apply it at
dusk, after foraging has stopped, so the residue is dry before bees return. This
is not a formality; spinosad applied to a flowering crop in the middle of a sunny
morning kills pollinators.

It is toxic to many predatory and parasitic insects too, so it is a tool for a
real infestation rather than a routine one.

## Spinosad and resistance

Spinosad has a single mode of action, and populations of some pests have already
developed resistance to it where it was used repeatedly as the only control.
Because organic growers have few alternatives, losing spinosad to resistance is
a serious and permanent cost.

Use it sparingly, never on consecutive generations of the same pest, and always
alongside non-chemical controls rather than in place of them.

## Pyrethrum is fast, broad and blunt

Pyrethrum is extracted from chrysanthemum flowers and attacks the insect nervous
system on contact. It is very fast — insects drop within minutes — and it breaks
down rapidly in sunlight, so it leaves little residue.

Its problem is that it is almost completely non-selective. It kills ladybirds,
lacewings, hoverflies, predatory bugs, parasitic wasps and bees as readily as the
target pest, and because it knocks down the visible adults it can look like a
great success while removing the natural control that was holding the population
down. Rebound infestations after pyrethrum are common and well documented.

It is also highly toxic to fish and other aquatic life, so it must be kept away
from ditches, ponds and watercourses.

## When pyrethrum is the right call

Rarely, and specifically: a severe infestation, on a crop with real value at
stake, where the alternatives have failed or the pest is not susceptible to
them, and where the block is not flowering. Treat it as an emergency measure
whose cost is the beneficial population, and plan for what happens after.

Note that some pyrethrum products are formulated with piperonyl butoxide, a
synergist that is not permitted under many organic standards. Always check the
product against your own certification body's rules, and follow the label.
