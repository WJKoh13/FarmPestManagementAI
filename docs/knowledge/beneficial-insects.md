## The predators worth recognising

Ladybirds are widely known as adults and almost universally destroyed as larvae.
The larva is the more voracious stage and looks nothing like the beetle: a small,
elongated, alligator-shaped, blue-grey creature with orange markings, moving
quickly over leaves. Farmers regularly report it as a pest.

Hoverfly larvae are legless, translucent green or brown maggots that lie among
aphid colonies and are easy to miss entirely; the adults look like small wasps
but hover and do not sting. Lacewing larvae are similar in shape to ladybird
larvae with large curved jaws. Ground beetles are fast, dark, shiny beetles that
run from a lifted stone and eat soil-dwelling larvae and eggs at night.

## Parasitic wasps and the mummies they leave

Most parasitic wasps in a crop are tiny — a few millimetres, often barely
visible — and they do not sting people. The female lays an egg inside a pest,
and the larva develops within it.

Their unmistakable sign is the aphid mummy: a swollen, papery, bronze or tan
aphid glued to the leaf, often with a neat round exit hole. A colony full of
mummies is a colony being destroyed without any intervention, and spraying it is
straightforwardly counterproductive. Learning to spot mummies is one of the
highest-value skills in this whole subject.

## Why spraying can make an infestation worse

Pest species usually breed faster than the things that eat them. After a
broad-spectrum spray both populations crash, but the pest recovers first, and it
recovers into a field with no predators left. The second peak is often higher
than the one that was sprayed — the standard name for this is pest resurgence.

This is the mechanism behind the observation that a farm which sprays routinely
often has worse pest problems than a neighbour who does not.

## Keeping them without buying them

Predators and parasitoids need three things the crop itself rarely provides:
nectar and pollen for the adults, shelter over winter, and alternative prey when
pest numbers are low.

Flowering strips, hedgerows, uncut margins, tolerance of some flowering weeds,
and simply not clearing every scrap of cover in autumn all supply these. Buying
in released predators is expensive and usually temporary; making the farm a
place they stay is neither.
