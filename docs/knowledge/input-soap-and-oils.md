## How they kill

Insecticidal soaps are potassium salts of fatty acids. They work physically:
the soap disrupts the waxy outer layer of a soft-bodied insect, which then
loses water and collapses. Horticultural oils work by a related physical route,
coating the insect and blocking the spiracles it breathes through.

Neither is a nerve poison and neither leaves a meaningful residue. That has one
enormous consequence for how they are used: **they only work while wet.** Once
the spray has dried, it has no further effect at all, and an insect landing on
the plant an hour later is completely unaffected.

## Why coverage decides everything

Because the mechanism is contact and momentary, the spray must physically touch
the insect. Not the leaf near the insect — the insect. Aphids on the underside
of a leaf, in a curled tip, or inside a whorl are simply not treated by a spray
directed at the canopy from above.

In practice this means turning leaves over, spraying from beneath where possible,
and accepting that a single pass will never reach everything. It also means these
products fail predictably against anything armoured, mobile, or sheltered, and
succeed against soft, slow, exposed pests like aphids on an open leaf.

## They are gentle afterwards and harsh at the moment

Having no residue makes soaps and oils among the safest options for beneficial
insects *over time* — a predatory wasp arriving the next day meets nothing. But
at the moment of spraying they are entirely indiscriminate. Any soft-bodied
beneficial that is wetted dies exactly as the aphid does, including ladybird
larvae and hoverfly larvae, which are usually the most valuable things in an
aphid-infested crop.

Spraying only the infested patch, rather than the whole block, preserves the
predator population that will do the rest of the work.

## Where they damage the crop

Soaps and oils can burn foliage, and the risk rises with heat, bright sun,
drought stress and certain sensitive species. The same physical action that
strips an insect's cuticle can strip a leaf's protective waxes.

Spraying in the cool of early morning or evening, on plants that are not
water-stressed, avoids most of this. Testing on a few plants and waiting a couple
of days before treating a whole block is worth the delay on any crop or variety
you have not sprayed before. Oils must not be combined with sulphur, and the two
should be well separated in time. Always follow the product label and the rules
of your certification body.
