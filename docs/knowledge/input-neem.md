## What the active ingredient actually does

Neem's main insecticidal compound is azadirachtin, extracted from the seed of
the neem tree. It is not a contact poison in the way most people expect. It
works mainly as an insect growth regulator and an antifeedant: treated insects
stop feeding, and immature ones fail to moult properly into their next stage.

That mechanism explains the single most common complaint about neem, which is
that "nothing happened". Nothing visibly happens for days. The insects are still
on the plant, and they are still alive, but they have stopped eating and will
not reach adulthood. Judging neem by the count of bodies the next morning will
always make it look like a failure.

## Why it works better on the young

Because the effect runs through moulting, neem hits nymphs and small larvae far
harder than adults. An adult beetle that has finished growing has no moult left
to disrupt, so it may feed less but will not die of it.

The practical consequence is that neem is a tool for early infestations. Once a
population is dominated by adults, neem is close to the wrong choice, and
waiting until damage is obvious usually guarantees that is the population you
have.

## Coverage is the whole game

Azadirachtin does not move through the plant to any useful degree and it breaks
down quickly in sunlight, so it only affects insects it actually lands on or
that eat treated tissue soon after. Aphids on the underside of a leaf are not
touched by a spray that only wetted the top.

This is why neem so often fails on sucking pests sheltering in curled leaves or
growing tips: the spray physically cannot reach them. If the pest is in a place
you cannot wet, neem is not the answer, whatever the label says.

## Effects on other insects

Neem is gentler than the broad-spectrum organic insecticides, but it is not
selective. It can harm predatory larvae and immature parasitic wasps, which are
also growing insects with moults to disrupt. It is toxic to bees while the spray
is still wet, though much less so once dry.

Spraying at dusk, when bees are not flying and the sun will not degrade it
immediately, addresses both the pollinator risk and the breakdown problem at
once. Always follow the product label and the rules of your certification body.
