## Diatomaceous earth abrades, so it must stay dry

Diatomaceous earth is the fossilised silica skeletons of microscopic algae.
Under a microscope the particles are sharp; against an insect they abrade the
waxy cuticle, and the insect dies of water loss. There is no poison and nothing
for a pest to develop resistance to.

The mechanism collapses the moment it gets wet. Dew, rain or irrigation turns
the dust into a harmless paste, and it must be reapplied once the foliage dries.
In a humid climate or under overhead irrigation this makes it close to useless
on leaves, though it remains practical as a dry barrier band around stems or in
a covered position.

Use the horticultural grade. The type sold for swimming-pool filters has been
heat-treated into a form that is a genuine respiratory hazard, and it does not
work any better.

## It does not know what it is killing

Any insect that crawls through it is abraded, including ground beetles and other
predators that patrol the soil surface, and it is harmful to bees if applied to
flowers. Band it where the pest travels rather than broadcasting it over
everything, and keep it off blossom.

Wear a dust mask when applying. Fine silica dust is unpleasant to inhale
regardless of grade.

## Kaolin clay works by disguise

Kaolin is a fine white clay sprayed as a slurry that dries to a powdery film
over the whole plant. It does not poison anything. It deters insects by making
the surface unrecognisable and physically awkward: the pest cannot identify the
plant as a host, cannot get a grip, and is irritated by particles sticking to it.

Because it is a film, it needs full coverage to work and needs renewing after
heavy rain and as new growth expands unprotected. A partial coat gives the pest
a clean landing site.

Kaolin also reflects sunlight, which reduces heat stress and sunburn on exposed
fruit — often a bigger benefit than the pest control on hot-climate crops.

## Where the film is a problem

The white residue is visible at harvest and has to be washed off produce sold
fresh, which can rule it out on a crop sold on appearance close to picking. It
can also interfere with the natural enemies that hunt by sight on the plant
surface, and heavy films can slightly reduce photosynthesis.

Neither dust is a rescue treatment. Both are preventive: they work when they are
already in place before the pest arrives, and they do very little once a
population is established. Always follow the product label and the rules of your
certification body.
