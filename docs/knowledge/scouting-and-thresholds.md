## How to walk a field

Walk a pattern, not the edges. A W or an X across the block, stopping at fixed
points, samples the middle of the crop where problems are usually found later
and are usually worse by then. Edges mislead in both directions: pests that
migrate in are concentrated there, and pests that emerge from the soil are not.

Look at the same number of plants at each stop, and look at the same parts of
each plant — undersides of leaves, growing tips, the stem at soil level, and the
soil itself around the base. Most pests spend most of their time somewhere a
casual glance does not reach, which is why the first sighting is so often the
damage rather than the animal.

Go at the right time of day. Many chewing pests feed at night and shelter in the
soil or leaf litter by mid-morning, so a field that looks clean at noon can be
busy at dusk.

## Counting beats impressions

Write down what you find, at each stop, on each date. The number itself matters
less than the direction it moves. Ten aphids per plant falling is a different
situation from three per plant rising, and no single visit can tell them apart.

Records also let you compare years, which is how you learn whether a rotation or
a habitat planting is actually working on your own ground rather than in theory.

## What an economic threshold is

A threshold is the pest level at which the expected crop loss exceeds the cost
of doing something about it. Below it, treating loses money — the damage would
have cost less than the intervention.

Thresholds are specific to the crop, the growth stage, the market and the
season, and published ones come from particular regions and production systems.
Use them as a way of thinking rather than as numbers to copy: a young transplant
can be killed outright by damage a mature plant would shrug off, and a crop sold
on appearance tolerates far less than one sold for processing.

## Damage is not the same as loss

A plant with chewed outer leaves may yield exactly as much as an untouched one.
Many crops compensate for early leaf loss by growing more leaf, and cosmetic
damage on parts nobody eats or sells costs nothing at all.

Ask what is actually being lost before treating: the growing point, the harvested
organ, the plant's ability to stand up, or only the appearance of a leaf that
will be discarded anyway.
