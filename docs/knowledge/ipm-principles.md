## Why the order matters more than the method

Integrated pest management is not a list of approved products. It is an order of
operations, and the order is what does most of the work. Prevention first, then
monitoring, then physical and biological controls, and only then anything
sprayed — even something organically approved.

Farmers who reverse this end up spraying more, not less, because a spray treats
the insects present today and does nothing about the conditions that brought
them. A field with no flowering margin, continuous host crops and no rotation
will refill with the same pest within a fortnight, and the second outbreak is
usually worse than the first because the natural enemies were sprayed too.

## Prevention is a planting decision

Most of what determines pest pressure happens before anything is in the ground:
what was grown in that field last year, whether the residue was worked in,
whether the variety has any resistance, when it was sown relative to the pest's
emergence, and how much habitat exists nearby for the predators that would
otherwise do the work for free.

Sowing date is the most underrated of these. Many pests have a narrow window
when they move onto a crop, and a planting shifted by two or three weeks can
miss it entirely. This costs nothing and is invisible in a spray record, which
is precisely why it gets overlooked.

## Monitoring turns a guess into a decision

Walking the crop on a schedule, looking in the same way each time, is what
separates a decision from a reaction. It tells you whether numbers are rising
or falling, which matters far more than the count on any single day, and it
catches an infestation while it is still confined to one corner.

It also tells you when *not* to act. A great deal of unnecessary spraying is
done against populations that were already collapsing under their own natural
enemies, and the spray then removes those enemies and guarantees a rebound.

## An organic spray is still a disruption

Approved-for-organic does not mean harmless. Several organic insecticides are
broad-spectrum: they kill predatory beetles, parasitic wasps and pollinators
along with the target. Treat any spray as a last resort with a real cost, not as
the natural end point of noticing a pest.
