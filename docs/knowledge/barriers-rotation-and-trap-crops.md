## Floating row covers

A lightweight fabric laid over a crop physically excludes flying pests. It is
the single most reliable control against pests that must arrive from outside,
because it does not depend on the pest's behaviour, the weather or the timing of
a spray.

It works only if it is sealed. Edges must be buried or weighted along their whole
length, and any gap is a doorway. It must also go on *before* the pest arrives —
a cover laid over an infestation is a greenhouse for it.

The critical exception is any crop that needs insect pollination. Covers must
come off at flowering, or the crop sets no fruit. For leaf and root crops that
never flower before harvest, they can stay on the whole season.

## Collars, bands and trenches

Cutworms sever seedlings at soil level, and a simple collar — a ring of card or
tin pushed a little into the soil around the stem — stops them physically. It is
laborious and it is close to absolute, which makes it worthwhile for a small
number of high-value transplants.

Sticky bands around stems intercept crawling pests, and steep-sided trenches
trap flightless species migrating on foot. None of these need a product, a
sprayer or a re-entry period.

## Rotation only works against the immobile

Rotation starves soil-dwelling pests with narrow host ranges by removing the
host from the ground they are in. It is genuinely effective against wireworms,
white grubs and other larvae that spend a year or more in the soil and cannot
travel far.

It does almost nothing against strong fliers. Moving a crop to the next field
does not inconvenience a moth. The rule of thumb is that rotation works in
inverse proportion to how far the pest can move — and its effect depends on the
distance between this year's crop and last year's ground, not merely on the
sequence written in a plan.

Rotating after grass or a grassy cover crop is a specific and common trap: those
are exactly the conditions that build up wireworm and grub populations, and the
following crop inherits them.

## Trap crops and the step everyone forgets

A trap crop is a more attractive planting used to draw a pest away from the main
crop — a border of a preferred host, sown early so it is at the stage the pest
wants when the pest arrives.

The step that gets missed is destroying it. A trap crop left standing becomes a
nursery: the pest breeds up in it and moves into the main crop in far greater
numbers than would ever have arrived naturally. It must be destroyed, or
treated, while the pests are still concentrated in it.
