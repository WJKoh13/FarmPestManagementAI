## The damage names the mouthparts

Most of the time the insect has gone and only the damage remains, and the damage
narrows the field considerably because it reflects how the animal eats.

Chewing insects remove tissue: ragged holes, notched leaf edges, whole leaves
stripped to the midrib, or a seedling cut clean through at the base. Caterpillars
and beetle larvae leave holes with irregular edges; adult beetles often leave
small round shot-holes or neat scallops from the margin inward.

Sucking insects remove sap and leave the leaf intact but damaged: pale stippling
or fine speckling, yellowing, curling and distortion of new growth, and sticky
honeydew that later blackens with sooty mould. If a leaf is misshapen but has no
holes in it, stop looking for a caterpillar.

Boring insects leave a small entry hole, often with sawdust-like frass at it, and
a wilting or broken stem above.

## Where on the plant, and how old

Damage concentrated on the newest growth points to something feeding on soft
tissue, often a sucking pest sheltering in the tips. Damage on old lower leaves
usually means something working up from the soil. Damage at soil level on a
seedling that has toppled means a cutworm or similar.

Age matters too. Old damage has dried, browned and hardened edges and may have
expanded as the leaf grew; fresh damage is moist and pale at the cut. A field
full of old damage and no new damage needs no treatment at all — a fact that
prevents a great many pointless sprays.

## Things that are not insects

A large share of suspected pest damage is not pest damage. Nutrient deficiency
produces yellowing in characteristic patterns, usually symmetrical and without
any tissue removed. Drought and heat scorch leaf margins. Wind abrades and tears.
Herbicide drift distorts new growth in a way that closely mimics sucking-pest
damage. Fungal and bacterial disease produces spots with defined margins, halos
or visible growth.

The distinguishing question is usually whether tissue has been physically
removed. If nothing has been eaten, an insecticide has nothing to do.

## When to go and look at night

If leaves are being visibly eaten and daytime searching finds nothing, the
feeder is almost certainly nocturnal. Cutworms, many caterpillars and several
beetles feed after dark and shelter in the soil, under debris or in leaf litter
by morning.

Going out with a torch after dark, or searching the top few centimetres of soil
around a damaged plant, resolves this far more often than any amount of daytime
inspection.
